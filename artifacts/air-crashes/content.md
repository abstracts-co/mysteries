## Malaysia Airlines 370, 8 March 2014

MH370 disappeared after leaving its route; its final location in the southern Indian Ocean remains unknown. Radar, satellite data, and confirmed debris provide clues, but the main wreckage and cause remain unknown.

Aboard: 239. Died: 239.

### Explanations

**Cause undetermined** (official finding; Malaysian ICAO Annex 13 Safety Investigation Team). The flight diverted and the initial turnback probably involved manual input. The investigation could not determine the cause, controller identity or motive, and did not establish a specific crash site.

**Unresponsive flight and fuel exhaustion** (credible alternative; ATSB search strategy working group, with Boeing, Inmarsat and DST Group). The search used an extended, largely stable southern flight ending near fuel exhaustion as its leading working scenario. A final loss and restoration of electrical power could explain the terminal log-on; this does not prove why the crew were unresponsive or when that began.

**Inmarsat illustrative path** (credible alternative; Chris Ashton and Inmarsat co-authors, 2014). The paper gives one path consistent with much of the timing and frequency evidence. The authors explicitly say many other routes fit and that its endpoint is uncertain.

**UGIB due-south route** (credible alternative; Bobby Ulich, Richard Godfrey, Victor Iannello and Andrew Banks, 2020; later Ulich and Iannello drift work). UGIB combines satellite data, aircraft performance, weather, fuel and drift evidence to favour a due-south late route. It proposes a low-altitude segment before the final southbound cruise; that detailed early path and claims of planned diversion are not official findings.

**CSIRO drift region** (official finding; CSIRO oceanographers David Griffin, Peter Oke and collaborators). CSIRO drift work favoured a region near 35 S, within a broader southern section of the seventh arc. Part III proposed a tighter location only if certain unconfirmed objects in satellite imagery were MH370 debris.

**Controlled final descent** (minority; Larry Vance; Vincent Lyne’s 2024 paper; some independent analysts). This view allows pilot control near the end, potentially a glide or controlled water entry farther from the seventh arc. Lyne argues that a coupled-motion model favours an eastward descent; it is a reinterpretation of the final signals, not evidence of an identified pilot action.

**WSPR tracking claim** (minority; Richard Godfrey and WSPR-method collaborators; opposed by Iannello, Ulich, Exner and other critics). Godfrey attributes anomalies in amateur-radio propagation reports to MH370 and infers a detailed route and later endpoint. Those reports do not directly identify an aircraft; this contested inference is excluded from the observed replay.

**Technical emergency and incapacitation** (credible alternative; Technical-failure or hypoxia scenario proponents; considered but unproven by the Malaysian investigation). A technical emergency followed by incapacitation could explain unanswered communications and an extended autopilot flight. The official investigation did not find a failure sequence that explained all the observed deactivations and route changes, but could not exclude every technical possibility.

**Java acoustic candidate** (minority; Ed Anderson; Victor Iannello recommended a limited check in March 2026). Anderson links an acoustic/seismic event to a possible MH370 seabed impact near Java. Iannello recommends checking the small candidate area while explicitly stating that the sound cannot be definitively linked to MH370.

**Remote takeover claim** (rejected; Some public remote-control takeover proponents; rejected by Malaysian investigators). This claim invokes Boeing’s patented remote-takeover technology as a way the aircraft could have been flown from the ground. Investigators found no evidence the proposed technology had been installed or used on 9M-MRO.

### What happened

- 2014-03-07 16:25:52 Airways clearance requested: The first officer requested the airways clearance for Beijing. Ground radio recordings, rather than a recovered CVR, preserve this exchange.
- 2014-03-07 16:27:37 Pushback and start requested: The crew requested pushback and engine start. No distress was reported.
- 2014-03-07 16:40:37 Cleared for takeoff: Lumpur Tower cleared MH370 for takeoff.
- 2014-03-07 16:42Z Departure recorded: The departure message records 16:42 UTC from Kuala Lumpur. The original ACARS series starts at 16:41:43; a precise wheels-off instant is not established here.
- 2014-03-07 16:42:53 Cleared direct to IGARI: Departure cleared the aircraft to FL180 and replaced the SID with direct IGARI. This was an assigned flight level, not a recorded instantaneous altitude.
- 2014-03-07 16:46:58 Cleared to flight level 250: The aircraft received clearance to climb to FL250.
- 2014-03-07 16:50:08 Cleared to flight level 350: The aircraft received clearance to climb to FL350.
- 2014-03-07 17:01:17 Crew reported cruise level: The crew reported maintaining FL350. The later ACARS batch contains pressure-altitude samples near that level.
- 2014-03-07 17:07:29 Last ACARS batch sent: The last position batch contained six five-minute samples ending at 17:06:43. The last sample recorded 43,800 kg of fuel; it is not the fuel quantity at the transmission instant.
- 2014-03-07 17:07:56 Cruise level reported again: The crew again reported maintaining FL350. No problem was announced.
- 2014-03-07 17:19:26 Handover to Ho Chi Minh: Lumpur Radar instructed MH370 to contact Ho Chi Minh on 120.9 MHz. No subsequent contact with that centre was established.
- 2014-03-07 17:19:30 Last recorded radio acknowledgement: The captain acknowledged the handover. This quote is from the official report’s reproduction of ground-recorded radio traffic; no cockpit recording has been recovered.
- 2014-03-07 17:20:30 Last decoded Kota Bharu reply: Independent decoding identifies the last Kota Bharu SSR reply at this time. It distinguishes received replies from the later disappearance of a coasted target on a display.
- 2014-03-07 17:20:31 Aircraft reached IGARI area: The official radar chronology says the aircraft passed IGARI. A timed passage near a waypoint does not make the waypoint an exact radar coordinate.
- 2014-03-07 17:20:34 Last decoded ADS-B report: The 2026 dataset ends ADS-B at about 6.93192 N, 103.59251 E. The final two messages omit the flight-level field; the data do not record a plunge to zero altitude.
- 2014-03-07 17:20:36 Mode S symbol disappeared: The official chronology records loss of the Mode S display symbol at this time. Display timing differs from the individual reply times decoded later.
- 2014-03-07 17:21:13 Secondary radar symbol disappeared: The final KL secondary-radar position symbol disappeared. Military radar subsequently traced the turnback; this display timestamp is not an exact timestamp for the transponder switch position.
- 2014-03-07 17:30:33 Primary return near Kota Bharu: A primary radar return associated with the returning aircraft appears in the independently decoded Kota Bharu log. It supplies position, not a trustworthy altitude.
- 2014-03-07 17:39:06 Controllers queried missing contact: Ho Chi Minh queried Kuala Lumpur about MH370. A later table in the same report gives 17:39:03; the three-second discrepancy is retained in conflicts.
- 2014-03-07 17:52:27 Phone registered near Penang: A mobile network recorded a hit from the first officer’s phone at Bandar Baru Farlim. No call or message was recorded, and subsequent tests did not establish the aircraft’s altitude from this hit.
- 2014-03-07 18:03:23 Airline message failed to arrive: Operations sent a cockpit-printer message asking the crew to contact Ho Chi Minh. Repeated attempts through 18:43:33 received no aircraft acknowledgement.
- 2014-03-07 18:22:12 Last military radar return: The official report places the last military radar return about 10 nautical miles beyond MEKAR. Published map coordinates differ between sources, and no later unique aircraft position is recorded.
- 2014-03-07 18:25:27 Satellite terminal logged on: The aircraft terminal initiated a log-on, ending the satellite-link outage. A power interruption is the leading official explanation; its cause is unknown.
- 2014-03-07 18:39:52 First satellite call went unanswered: The ground station began call setup. The aircraft terminal exchanged signalling but the call was not answered.
- 2014-03-07 19:41:02 Satellite handshake 2: The terminal responded with BTO 11500 microseconds and BFO 111 Hz. This constrains an arc, not a unique position.
- 2014-03-07 20:41:04 Satellite handshake 3: The terminal responded with BTO 11740 microseconds and BFO 141 Hz. This constrains an arc, not a unique position.
- 2014-03-07 21:30Z Rescue coordination centre activated: Kuala Lumpur’s rescue coordination centre was activated after the prolonged loss of contact. The investigation criticised delays in alerting and coordination.
- 2014-03-07 21:41:26 Satellite handshake 4: The terminal responded with BTO 12780 microseconds and BFO 168 Hz. This constrains an arc, not a unique position.
- 2014-03-07 22:32Z Distress phase declared: Kuala Lumpur transmitted the DETRESFA message. This was an administrative distress declaration, not a distress transmission from the aircraft.
- 2014-03-07 22:41:21 Satellite handshake 5: The terminal responded with BTO 14540 microseconds and BFO 204 Hz. This constrains an arc, not a unique position.
- 2014-03-07 23:13:58 Second satellite call went unanswered: A second ground-initiated telephone call produced signalling but no answered call. Voice-channel BFO is available; voice-channel BTO is not.
- 2014-03-08 00:10:59 Sixth arc response received: The sixth handshake response had BTO 18,040 microseconds and BFO 252 Hz. Rounded publications often label this exchange 00:11 UTC.
- 2014-03-08 00:19:29 Final log-on request received: The terminal initiated another log-on. Raw BTO was 23,000 microseconds and BFO 182 Hz; the standard log-on correction gives BTO 18,400 microseconds.
- 2014-03-08 00:19:37 Last aircraft transmission received: The last received aircraft message had BFO -2 Hz. Models interpret the sharp frequency change as a rapid descent, but it does not directly record impact, attitude, control status or one exact descent rate.
- 2014-03-08 01:15:56 Later interrogation received no reply: The ground station began another interrogation sequence and received no response. There is no eighth completed handshake.
-  Southern Indian Ocean conclusion announced: Satellite analysis led Malaysian authorities to conclude that the flight ended in the southern Indian Ocean. It did not establish a crash coordinate.
-  Aerial search ended: The southern Indian Ocean aerial search concluded without recovering confirmed MH370 debris. The search then shifted toward the seabed.
-  Flaperon found on Réunion: A right flaperon was found on Réunion. Investigators subsequently confirmed it came from MH370.
-  Left flap fragment found: A left outboard flap fragment was found at Ilot Bernache, Mauritius. It was later confirmed as MH370 debris.
-  Right flap found near Tanzania: A right outboard flap section was found on Pemba Island. Its identifying numbers linked it to MH370.
-  Review proposed another search area: The First Principles Review recommended about 25,000 additional square kilometres near the seventh arc, north of much of the earlier search. This was a probability-based recommendation.
-  Government-led underwater search suspended: The tripartite search was suspended without locating the main wreckage after more than 120,000 square kilometres of high-resolution search. Coverage was extensive but not an assurance about the entire Indian Ocean.
-  ATSB operational report released: The ATSB documented the search and its limits. Search findings did not determine the accident’s cause.
-  Ocean Infinity began seabed search: Ocean Infinity began searching the identified area under an agreement signed on 10 January. It later widened its survey.
-  Ocean Infinity search ended: Ocean Infinity ended its search without finding the aircraft, reporting more than 112,000 square kilometres surveyed. That area overlaps earlier coverage.
-  Safety report left cause undetermined: Malaysia publicly released its safety report. It found that the turnback likely involved manual input but could not establish who controlled the aircraft or the reason for the disappearance.
-  WSPR endpoint claim published: Godfrey published a WSPR-based endpoint claim. It remains contested by radio and satellite-data analysts and is not used as observed track in this brief.
-  Tracking safety follow-up finalised: The ATSB finalised its aircraft-tracking safety issue while continuing to encourage shorter tracking intervals and autonomous distress-tracking retrofits.
-  Alternative descent paper published: Lyne published a peer-reviewed reinterpretation favouring a controlled eastward descent. It reanalyses the same final satellite messages and provides no new confirmed wreckage position.
-  New search approval announced: The minister announced agreement in principle to a renewed Ocean Infinity search. The signed contract and the start of contracted operations followed later; this announcement alone did not establish that surveying had begun.
-  New search agreement signed: Malaysia signed an agreement for an estimated 15,000-square-kilometre search area. Ocean Infinity had also undertaken some surveying before this contract date.
-  First contracted phase ended: The first contracted phase ran from 25 to 28 March 2025. The government’s later total includes only 28 operational days across both contracted phases.
-  Second contracted phase began: The government dates the second contracted search phase from 31 December 2025. This is distinct from a vessel’s earlier mobilisation or announced planned start.
-  Vessel left search area: Ocean Infinity left the area without confirmed wreckage findings. Malaysia later reported about 7,571 square kilometres surveyed within the defined area across the two contracted phases.
-  Search results reported to families: The government and Ocean Infinity reported no confirmed location of MH370. The company’s total of more than 140,000 square kilometres is cumulative since 2018.
-  Search agreement extension announced: The minister’s statement announced extension through 30 June 2027, with 7,428.54 square kilometres remaining. November 2026 to April 2027 was described as an asset-deployment window, not a confirmed restart date.
-  Updated radar dataset documented: The independent ATC-Caption dataset documentation reached version 1.7, with a version 2 archive. Its source data distinguish actual SSR/ADS-B replies from later display coasting.

### Not known

- Main wreckage location, impact coordinate, water depth, impact UTC time and disposition of the aircraft. Government and operator sources through 2026 report no confirmed location.
- FDR and CVR contents: neither recorder recovered. There are no actual final-minute FDR pitch, roll, angle-of-attack, engine or configuration tables to extract.
- Why communications ceased, what caused the early satellite power interruption, who controlled the aircraft, whether any unlawful interference occurred, and motive. No allegation against an individual is established here.
- Exact turnback path and altitude during the gap between last SSR/ADS-B and returning primary-radar coverage. Dense later plots do not fill this gap.
- True altitude above mean sea level. Pressure altitude/flight levels and GNSS ellipsoid height are available before signal loss but not converted without a defensible datum and atmospheric model. alt_ft therefore stays null; source pressure and ellipsoid heights remain in explicit extra fields.
- Reliable post-diversion altitude and military point-to-point speeds. The official report warns that these outputs are erroneous; independent proposed climbs and descents are hypotheses.
- Exact point and time of the final south turn, navigation/autopilot modes, later manoeuvres and any post-00:19 recovery or glide. Satellite arcs alone do not specify these.
- Exact wind, cloud types/bases/tops, visibility, turbulence, illumination, moon, sea state and wave height at impact. The location/time are not known, and nearby weather profiles cannot be substituted for an impact observation.
- Exact wheels-off second. The official departure message gives 16:42 UTC and the ACARS sample starts at 16:41:43; these are distinct records.
- A verified manufacturer numeric wing-sweep value in the primary material read. wing_sweep_deg is null; no value is guessed from a drawing.
- Exact loss-date decorative livery decals. The inspected photograph establishes base colours, but its older Visit Malaysia Year marking is not dated to March 2014.
- Exact authoritative seafloor search polygons and per-AUV actual coverage for Ocean Infinity’s 2018 and 2025–2026 operations. Published area totals and approximate overview extents do not establish scanned footprints; missing geometries remain null.
- Exact authoritative 2025 contract polygon vertices. The 2024 proposal display corridor is an explicitly calculated approximation and cannot be substituted for the signed 15,000 km² area.
- Whether operations actually resumed by 7 November 2026. June’s extension and November–April deployment window are verified in a visually inspected reproduction of the minister’s statement and contemporary news reporting, but no opened primary release confirms an actual November restart.
- Exact UTC instants for shoreline recoveries, public report releases and contract-phase calendar dates. Source dates are retained without inventing midnight; find dates are not beaching dates.
- Whether the unconfirmed Pleiades-image objects or the Java acoustic event were related to MH370. Neither is labelled an identified aircraft detection.
- A defensible unique per-vertex time for Geoscience Australia’s military path geometry. The independent spreadsheet’s estimated times are explicitly unsuitable for calculating inter-point speeds, so those times were not used.
- A verified complete time-position table for Godfrey’s WSPR route. Only the opened, attributed endpoint claim is retained as a minority hypothesis; its claimed 130 positions are not invented or promoted to radar.
- Direct retrieval of some ATSB PDFs timed out in curl/requests; their accessible web PDF text was read instead. The Malaysian report, raw satellite log, Boeing geometry, original independent datasets and UGIB reports were downloaded and inspected locally.

## Amelia Earhart, 2 July 1937

The pilot and navigator disappeared after failing to locate Howland Island on their flight from Lae. Radio contacts document the failed approach, but the crew’s final location and what happened afterward remain unknown.

Aboard: 2. Died: 2.

### Explanations

**Fuel exhaustion near Howland** (official finding; 1937 U.S. Navy search command; Elgen Long; Nauticos and its radio-analysis team). The crew failed to find Howland, exhausted usable fuel and came down in the ocean. The 1937 search report inferred loss at sea; Long and Nauticos later developed more specific endurance and search-area reconstructions that remain unconfirmed.

**Nikumaroro reef landing** (minority; TIGHAR, including Ric Gillespie and Thomas King; Richard Jantz for the bone interpretation; ALI and PRF expedition proponents). The aircraft continued southeast and landed on Gardner's reef, leaving the crew as castaways. Documentary, radio, archaeological and photographic arguments make this a serious investigated alternative, but no recovered item has established that the crew or aircraft was there.

**Japanese capture** (rejected; Fred Goerner and later proponents including Mike Campbell; a separate 2017 History Channel photograph claim). Variants propose a landing in the Marshall Islands, Japanese custody and deaths on Saipan, sometimes with a secret mission or cover-up. The official investigations did not substantiate these claims, and the best-known photographic claim predates the disappearance.

**Taraia lagoon aircraft claim** (rejected; Archaeological Legacy Institute and Purdue Research Foundation expedition proponents, 2025–2026). The Taraia Object was proposed as Electra wreckage carried into Nikumaroro's lagoon. The 2026 expedition tested the proposal and reported that the anomaly was likely tree debris with a carbonate veneer.

### What happened

- 1937-07-02 00:00Z Departure from Lae: The aircraft left the old coastal airfield with the pilot and navigator. The official search report equates 1000 Lae local time with 0000 UTC on July 2.
- 1937-07-02 00:00Z Late weather reaches Lae: Chater says new weather information arrived as the aircraft was taking off. It forecast stronger easterly winds and local rain squalls; later radio broadcasts do not prove that the crew received it.
- 1937-07-02 04:18Z First clear Lae report: Chater records a relayed height of 7000 feet and speed of 140 knots. The kind of speed was not specified.
- 1937-07-02 05:19Z Cumulus and altitude report: Chater gives 10000 feet and a questionable coordinate notation. Collopy says a descent was intended because of thick cumulus.
- 1937-07-02 07:18Z Last report heard at Lae: Chater gives 8000 feet, above cumulus, and wind 23 knots. The report's coordinates and Collopy's different altitude are retained as conflicts.
- 1937-07-02 10:30Z Nauru relays ship sighting: A July 3 Sydney telegram says Nauru had heard a ship-in-sight report at 2030 Sydney time on July 2. It identifies Myrtlebank retrospectively; this does not establish an aircraft position or a visual identification of the aircraft from Nauru.
- 1937-07-02 14:15Z Itasca hears unreadable voice: The dedicated operator attributed a signal through static to the Earhart aircraft during 0245–0248 ship time. No useful position was obtained.
- 1937-07-02 15:15Z Listening schedule heard: The log records that Earhart would listen on the hour and half hour on 3105 kHz. Receipt times vary slightly between operators and later summaries.
- 1937-07-02 16:23Z Partly cloudy fragment logged: The dedicated log associates a partly-cloudy fragment with reception during 0453–0500 ship time. The other operator describes unreadable reception; later accounts expanded the weather description.
- 1937-07-02 17:44Z Crew asks for radio bearing: Earhart asked for a bearing on 3105 kHz and said she would whistle into the microphone. The next entry estimates a distance of about 200 miles.
- 1937-07-02 18:15Z Another bearing request: The crew asked Itasca to take a bearing and report it in half an hour. The following entry says about 100 miles out, without a direction.
- 1937-07-02 18:48Z Itasca explains bearing limitation: Itasca transmitted that it could not take a bearing well on 3105 and requested 500 kHz, or asked whether Earhart wanted to take a bearing on the ship. The log gives no answer.
- 1937-07-02 19:10Z Fuel warning differs between logs: The general-purpose log records a claim of only half an hour of fuel left at 0740. The dedicated log at 0742 instead says fuel was running low, so a precise fuel reserve cannot be recovered from this evidence.
- 1937-07-02 19:12Z Island remains out of sight: Earhart reported believing they were near the ship but could not see it, and gave 1000 feet as their altitude. Itasca logged a strong signal but had no aircraft position.
- 1937-07-02 19:28Z Circling and requesting a signal: Earhart reported circling and asked for a signal on 7500 kHz. Itasca transmitted on that frequency.
- 1937-07-02 19:30Z Signal heard but bearing fails: The original log records that the aircraft received the ship's signals but could not obtain a minimum for direction finding. Itasca again requested transmissions that its own equipment could use.
- 1937-07-02 20:13Z Final call in the rough log: The original rough log gives a 157/337 line and a planned repeat on 6210 kHz, without a reference point. It places a questioned north/south addendum with that entry; the later Lexington report separates a 20:25 UTC call, so the chronology is disputed.
- 1937-07-02 20:14Z Itasca listens on another frequency: Operators listened on 6210 kHz and tried to keep Earhart on 3105. No further intelligible aircraft reply appears in these entries.
- 1937-07-02 20:25Z Later report lists another call: Noyes' July 29 Lexington report separately lists a north/south message at 20:25 UTC. The original rough log instead puts a questioned addendum with its 20:13 entry; this later-report event is retained as a conflict, not confirmation of an additional transmission.
- 1937-07-02 22:10Z Itasca begins sea search: At 1040 ship time Itasca left its homing position to search. Its initial northern search sector followed assumptions about visibility and navigation, not a measured aircraft track.
-  Unverified signals influence the search: During July 3–6, reported distress signals prompted searches west and north of Howland and later toward the Phoenix Islands. A report placing the aircraft 281 miles north was not a verified fix.
-  Gardner receives an aerial search: On July 9, three Colorado aircraft searched Gardner, now Nikumaroro. Lambrecht described signs of recent habitation, but repeated circling brought no response and no Electra was identified.
-  The official search ends: The Navy search ended on July 18. The report inferred the fliers were lost at sea after searching several competing areas; it recovered neither the aircraft nor its occupants.
-  A reef object is photographed: On October 15, 1937, a photograph recorded an object near Gardner's northwest reef. Its later interpretation as Electra landing gear is disputed and is not a recovered component.
-  A captivity allegation is filed: A January 7, 1939 intelligence report preserved an anonymous bottle-message allegation about the Marshalls. Recording the allegation did not verify its author or identify the missing crew.
-  Bones found on Gardner: In 1940, colonial officials investigated a partial skeleton and associated objects found on Gardner. The discovery was real, but its exact location and personal identity were not established to modern standards.
-  Hoodless examines the remains: On April 4, 1941, the Suva examiner assessed the remains as male. The report and some measurements survive; the bones have not been located for modern testing.
-  Japan reports a negative investigation: A Tokyo-to-State telegram dated August 10, 1960 says Japan's investigation found no basis for execution-on-Saipan allegations. This weakens that claim but does not locate the aircraft.
-  Anthropologists defend the original assessment: Cross and Wright's 2015 paper preferred Hoodless' male identification over the 1998 reassessment. They also acknowledged that missing bones prevented a definitive answer.
-  Jaluit photograph predates the flight: In July 2017, a library publication date showed that the photograph used to support a captivity claim had appeared in a 1935 book. It cannot depict the missing crew after July 1937.
-  Jantz publishes a different bone analysis: The 2018 study found close similarity between surviving bone measurements and estimates of Earhart's proportions. It provided statistical support for the castaway hypothesis, not a DNA or individual identification.
-  Sextant-box evidence changes: In October 2018, archival research connected the relevant sextant number with USS Bushnell. A TIGHAR archaeologist acknowledged that this weakened his earlier attribution of the box to Noonan.
-  Ballard search finds no aircraft parts: The August 2019 expedition searched Nikumaroro's reef slopes and deeper areas without identifying Electra components. Its published account explicitly leaves some areas and the wider hypothesis unresolved.
-  Sonar target attracts attention: In January 2024, Deep Sea Vision publicized a target from its 2023 survey and suggested it could be the Electra. The sonar shape alone did not establish an aircraft identity.
-  Sonar target proves to be rock: In November 2024, Deep Sea Vision reported that its target was a natural rock formation. The widely circulated January image is not a verified Electra wreck.
-  Purdue backs a lagoon inspection: On July 2, 2025, PRF and ALI proposed an expedition to test whether the Taraia image anomaly was aircraft wreckage. Permitting and seasonal considerations postponed the proposed 2025 work until 2026; the announcement itself was not a discovery.
-  More archival records become available: National Archives released digitized files in November and December 2025, followed by January 2026 additions. The release index supplies access to historical evidence, not a finding of a new crash site.
-  Taraia aircraft claim fails field inspection: The expedition's October 2026 statement said the anomaly was likely large trees with a carbonate coating. Other recovered objects were not authenticated as Electra parts; the sponsors continue to support the broader castaway hypothesis.

### Not known

- No flight-data recorder, cockpit voice recording, radar track or recovered onboard navigation log supplies a continuous track. The ten points are sparse observations and one explicit later-report coordinate rendering, not an interpolatable measured flight path.
- No confirmed wreck, identifiable crew remains, crash coordinates, depth, impact time, impact attitude, injury sequence or survival duration has been established in the opened sources through 2026-11-07. The two deaths are presumed; no successful escape or later survival is authenticated.
- Beyond the approximate departure airfield, no independently verified geographic fix at a known aircraft observation time was obtained. The Lae reports and later degree/minute renderings conflict; the 07:20 coordinate point is explicitly a later official report, not a second confirmed fix. Distance and radio strength do not locate the aircraft.
- The precise takeoff runway point and 1937 field elevation are unverified. Destination coordinates identify Howland generally, not an airfield threshold or the ship. Historical chart coordinates must not displace modern terrain without an explicit datum treatment.
- The 140- and 150-knot statements lack an IAS/TAS/groundspeed reference and may contain a unit misunderstanding. IAS, groundspeed and Mach therefore remain null.
- Reported heights are not recorder measurements. Altimeter setting, calibration, actual MSL error and continuous climb/descent profile are unknown; no vertical speeds are calculated between sparse reports.
- Fuel loaded is reported as approximately 1100 U.S. gallons by Lae accounts, but actual tank-by-tank usable fuel, mixture, power settings, consumption, winds encountered and fuel remaining late in the flight are not known. No exact exhaustion time is justified.
- The 157/337 orientation has no surviving reference point, along-line position or confirmed heading. The rough log questions the north/south addendum. No measured turn toward Nikumaroro or the Marshalls is preserved.
- Precise pitch, roll, angle of attack, flap settings, gear movements, engine RPM, manifold pressure, temperatures and failure indications during the final leg were not located. Do not animate specific attitudes or engine failures as facts.
- The exact fault behind failed communications and direction finding is unresolved. Photographic arguments for lost belly-antenna masts do not establish a complete failure mechanism or its time. A successful 7500-kHz reception did not yield a bearing.
- Original Nauru station logs and the original Sydney-to-State July 3 telegram image were not located in this review. The ship-sighting account comes from Jacobson's documented archival transcription; it is not an independently confirmed Nauru overflight or ship identification.

## Flight 19, 5 December 1945

Five training aircraft disappeared after navigation trouble; a search aircraft was also lost. The final locations and loss sequence remain unknown. The Navy later cleared the flight leader of blame.

Aboard: 27. Died: 27.

### Explanations

**Navigation loss and fuel exhaustion** (official finding; Original Navy Board of Investigation and 24 January 1946 reviewing authority; conventional explanation retained by many historians). The formation became disoriented over the Bahamas and Atlantic, flew changing courses and eventually made forced water landings in darkness after the last call. The original Board assigned the leader faulty judgment and found that communications and rescue coordination failed; the personal-blame finding was later disapproved.

**Cause unknown after correction** (official finding; Board for Correction of Naval Records, 28 October 1947; confirmed by Navy Office of Information, 18 November 1960). The correction board concluded that the flight disappeared for reasons unknown and disapproved the earlier blame finding. It considered that an unexpected development of weather may have occurred despite the leader’s efforts to return.

**Overland and Everglades reconstruction** (rejected; Andy Marocco and Jon Myhre, 2013–April 2014 version; wreck identification withdrawn by Marocco in April 2014). This reconstruction identified an inland radar formation as Flight 19 and proposed a southbound flight to an Everglades wreck. Its claimed identification of that wreck as FT-28 was withdrawn when the researcher identified a separate 1947 accident.

**Bermuda Triangle forces** (fringe; Some Bermuda Triangle writers and popular fiction; the museum lists an electromagnetic-disturbance theory, and NOAA documents alien, vortex, magnetic and methane speculation). These claims attribute the losses to an unusual regional force or a paranormal removal of the aircraft. No inspected primary record identifies such a force or provides measurements supporting one.

**Separate Mariner fire or explosion** (official finding; Original Navy Board for the probable loss event; tanker witnesses and USS Solomons radar report; later conventional histories). The Board associated Training 49 with the observed flames and radar disappearance near 28°59′N 80°25′W. It concluded an explosion at sea for unknown reasons; a particular fuel-system defect or vapour ignition source is not demonstrated by the inspected evidence.

- 1945-12-10 14:30:00 Board proceedings open in Miami: The Board began its hearings at NAS Miami on 10 December 1945 under a convening order of 7 December. Its later opinion attributed the loss to navigational confusion and faulty judgment, while documenting substantial rescue-system failures.
- 1945-12-10 20:27:00 Search termination is communicated: The base log records a telephone message that the search would be abandoned when that afternoon’s searches finished. It says search secured at night; 1527 is the notification, not the last aircraft landing.
-  Navy approves findings and recommends drills: The 24 January 1946 endorsement approved the proceedings and recommended frequent air-sea rescue drills to improve understanding among units. This is a documented recommendation; completion of those drills was not established here.
-  Correction board rejects blame: The 28 October 1947 correction letter disapproved the earlier opinion assigning blame to the leader and concluded that the flight disappeared for reasons or causes unknown. It also suggested unexpected weather may have intervened.
-  Navy confirms unknown cause: An 18 November 1960 Navy information letter confirmed the 1947 clearing of blame and said the cause remained unknown. Its compressed chronology conflicts with the original logs.
-  Everglades identification is withdrawn: In April 2014 the researcher who proposed that a Broward County wreck was FT-28 withdrew that identification. He attributed the wreck to BuNo 53118 and a separate 16 March 1947 accident.
-  Searchers discuss a different discovery: The Intrepid Museum’s October 2025 release described Flight 19 searchers finding a Challenger fragment in 2022. It did not announce recovery of Flight 19 or Training 49.
-  Anniversary review reports no confirmed trace: A December 2025 Smithsonian history reported that no confirmed trace of the six aircraft had been found. Searches for 2024–7 November 2026 developments found no opened official announcement establishing a Flight 19 recovery.

### Not known

- Exact final positions, water-landing or impact times, sequence of losses, survival intervals and seabed depths of all five Avengers remain unknown. No pinpoint disappearance site is supplied.
- No recovered FDR or CVR data exist in the inspected record. Base testimony says it had no voice recorder. These radio logs are partial written records; audio dramatizations cannot serve as cockpit recordings.
- No confirmed Avenger ground-speed, indicated airspeed, Mach, vertical-speed, pitch, bank, angle-of-attack, flap, gear or engine instrument time series was found. Type performance figures cannot fill these fields.
- The few recalled or logged altitude reports, 2300, 4500 and angles 3.5/3500 feet, lack verified altimeter setting and MSL datum. The first two also lack reliable transmission timestamps; alt_ft is null rather than invented MSL data.
- The 030/090/270-degree statements mix intended course, commanded turn and reported heading. Their true/magnetic reference and exact flown durations are unknown; no continuous path is integrated from them.
- The island or islands the flight leader saw were not positively identified. The Keys belief cannot be used as an actual position; the Board regarded a location south or west of Florida as improbable.
- Individual waypoint arrival times, the exact point of initial navigational error, the original student leading and the precise transfer of lead to the Marine captain were not established. The Board says the captain eventually led west before 1806R but gives no exact time.
- Compass failure mechanisms were not established. No recovered instrument proves failure, and the flight leader’s report is not evidence that all ten compasses in the formation failed.
- Actual fuel at each late transmission and consumption during the exercise are unknown. Testimony says planes were fully serviced, but inspection forms did not record quantities; estimated endurance is not measured fuel exhaustion.
- Precise weather, cloud tops, visibility, lightning, wave height, temperature, moon phase and illumination at the missing formation and at its final loss remain unknown. Shore weather and later rescue observations do not supply them.
- The preflight winds sheet says 1300 EWT. Surface forms and the operational record use Eastern standard/R time with UTC+5 conversion. The legacy EWT label was not silently converted into an exact UTC time.
- Exact takeoff runway thresholds and airfield elevations in 1945 were not established. The Board departure datum differs from the weather-station location. Barometer heights are not substituted for airport elevations; contemporary IATA/ICAO codes were not assigned retrospectively.
- The original navigation chart abbreviates route distances as miles/M and the finding does not expand the unit. Illustrative waypoint calculations explicitly assume nautical miles. Exact surveyed triangle vertices or digitized waypoint coordinates were not located.
- The HF/DF circle has no documented statistical confidence level. Its radius is printed as 100 miles without a unit expansion; this JSON preserves that wording rather than forcing an exact kilometre radius. It must not be rendered as a single precise aircraft fix.
- The unidentified inland radar formation was not proved to be Flight 19. Raw Solomons radar samples or a complete plot allowing independent identification were not located.
- The Mariner’s initiating failure, exact wreck identity/location, explosion altitude and impact attitude are unknown. A particular vapour leak or ignition source was not established. Reported flame height is not aircraft altitude.
- A chart depth of 78 ft was mentioned in testimony for the probable Mariner area, but no measured wreck depth was found. Debris/oily-film reports were not identified to BuNo 59225, and no final laboratory result was located.

- The actual 1947 Navy accident-card scan underlying the proponent’s identification of Everglades BuNo 53118 was not independently inspected. The withdrawal is certain as a published proponent statement; the wreck identification remains attributed to him.
- Searches included 2024, 2025 and 2026 developments through 7 November 2026. Opened 2025 museum and Smithsonian sources show continued interest and no confirmed recovery; no opened official new recovery or new physical identification was found. ## TWA 800, 18 July 1996

A fuel tank explosion broke apart a Boeing 747 shortly after takeoff from New York. Investigators established a fuel tank explosion and ruled out a missile or bomb. The exact ignition source remains unknown.

Aboard: 230. Died: 230.

### Explanations

**Center tank explosion** (official finding; NTSB Board, supported by its structures, fire/explosion and performance work). A flammable fuel/air mixture exploded in the center wing tank and initiated structural breakup. Excess voltage entering through fuel-quantity indication wiring was the most likely ignition route considered, but the actual initiating fault was not established.

**External explosion or missile** (rejected; TWA 800 Project petitioners, including some former investigation participants; other missile-theory advocates). The petitioners argue that radar debris motion, rising-light accounts, fractures and residue point to an external high-velocity explosion. A missile is a proposed mechanism among advocates; the petition did not establish a unique launch site, weapon, perpetrator or reproducible flight path.

**Onboard bomb** (rejected; FBI and NTSB as an early investigative possibility; later terrorism advocates). A bomb inside the aircraft was investigated as a possible trigger for the sudden breakup. Early responsibility claims and trace residues were leads, not a demonstrated causal chain.

**CIA witness reconstruction** (official finding; CIA analysts assisting the FBI; publicly presented in November 1997). CIA analysts built an independent model to explain how burning aircraft wreckage could appear as a rising streak. Its animation was an interpretation of witness perception and estimated aircraft motion; it was not the NTSB's definitive measured trajectory.

### What happened

- 1996-07-17 20:31:00 Previous flight lands: The aircraft arrived at JFK from Athens as TWA flight 881 at about 16:31 EDT. Its incoming crew reported no operational abnormality.
- 1996-07-17 23:59:44 Baggage concern resolved: Gate staff advised that the passenger associated with a previously removed bag had been on board. Ground equipment and the baggage check had delayed departure.
- 1996-07-18 00:01:42 Crew requests pushback: The crew requested pushback; physical pushback occurred about 20:02 EDT. Air-conditioning packs had operated on the ground before departure.
- 1996-07-18 00:08:13 Crew requests taxi: After starting engines 1, 2 and 4, the crew contacted ground control for taxi clearance.
- 1996-07-18 00:14:29 Fourth engine started: The crew started engine 3 during taxi and completed the delayed-start checks.
- 1996-07-18 00:17:18 Cleared onto departure runway: ATC cleared the aircraft to position and hold on runway 22R, with a wake-turbulence caution for a Boeing 757.
- 1996-07-18 00:18:21 Takeoff clearance: ATC cleared takeoff on runway 22R and reported wind from 240° at 8 knots. The statement is a tower report; it is not the offshore wind.
- 1996-07-18 00:19:00 Aircraft becomes airborne: FDR and CVR place liftoff at about 20:19 EDT. This timestamp is rounded, not an exact wheel-off measurement.
- 1996-07-18 00:20:14 Departure control contacted: The crew contacted Kennedy departure control during the initial climb.
- 1996-07-18 00:23:37 Handoff to Boston Center: The crew was transferred to Boston Center as the climb continued.
- 1996-07-18 00:25:41 Higher altitude assigned: Boston Center instructed a climb to 19,000 ft and expedited passage through 15,000 ft. The crew acknowledged.
- 1996-07-18 00:26:24 Clearance amended to thirteen thousand: ATC amended the clearance to maintain 13,000 ft MSL.
- 1996-07-18 00:27:47 Altitude alert sounds: The CVR recorded an altitude alert. FDR data showed the aircraft reaching its assigned altitude.
- 1996-07-18 00:29:15 Fuel flow reading questioned: The captain drew attention to an unusual engine 4 fuel-flow indication. This did not establish the later ignition mechanism.
- 1996-07-18 00:30:15 Climb to fifteen thousand cleared: Boston Center cleared a climb to 15,000 ft MSL. The crew called for climb thrust.
- 1996-07-18 00:30:19 Last radio acknowledgment: The right-seat captain/check airman acknowledged the climb clearance. The CVR clock gives 20:30:19.2; Boston Center gives 20:30:19.4 after correlation.
- 1996-07-18 00:30:35 Climb power set: The flight engineer reported the power setting complete. Recorded engine EPR subsequently rose as the aircraft climbed.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:11 Last Islip secondary return: The final Islip entry in the witness radar table reported X 19.5121 nm east and Y 8.6881 nm south of its antenna. Its altitude code was 137. Other radar sites continued briefly.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:11 Last FDR data word: The revised recorder analysis places the last recorded data word here. The investigators bounded power loss between this time and 00:31:12.89 UTC; the table must not be extended with the previous flight's data.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:12 Last transponder return: The last secondary return across the radar sources was recorded by the Trevose site at about 20:31:12 EDT. Subsequent primary echoes lacked transponder identity and altitude.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:12 Center wing tank explodes: The NTSB placed the initiating explosion in the center wing tank. This is a physical-evidence finding timed to recorder termination, not a recorder measurement of ignition or internal pressure.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:12 Cockpit recording ends: A loud transient lasting about 0.117 second preceded loss of the CVR recording. Brief changes in electrical background noise on the captain's channel occurred within the last second; they do not uniquely identify the ignition fault.
-  Forward fuselage separates: Published NTSB models place nose separation about 3–5 seconds after the initial event. This range would fall near 00:31:15–17 UTC, but no exact separation instant was recorded.
-  Main body pitches upward: NTSB simulations show the remaining aircraft briefly climbing and changing direction after losing the nose. Altitude, attitude and timing after the cutoff are model outputs, with several acceptable trajectories.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:46 Outboard wings separate in model: The final NTSB timing model assumes outboard wing failure 34 seconds after the initial event. This was inferred from wreckage, witnesses and modelling; it was not a recorded failure time.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:50 Nearby pilot reports explosion: The captain of Eastwind flight 507 reported seeing an explosion. This is the radio report time, not a reliable timestamp for the initial tank explosion.
- 1996-07-18 00:31:50 Center section fails in model: The final model assumes center-section failure adjacent to the left wing 38 seconds after the initial event. The resulting pieces follow ballistic paths rather than a controllable aircraft trajectory.
-  Main wreckage reaches the ocean: The NTSB estimated 47–54 seconds from the initiating explosion to main-body water impact, approximately 00:31:59–00:32:06 UTC. The forward fuselage impacted separately; no single exact time or point is established for all wreckage.
-  Search and recovery begin: On the evening of 17 July local time, aircraft and boats responded to the burning wreckage. No survivors were found.
-  Recorders recovered: Both recorders were recovered on 24 July 1996. The FDR arrived at the NTSB laboratory early on 25 July and yielded readable tape. Exact recovery UTC times are not supplied here.
-  FBI ends active criminal investigation: On 18 November 1997, the FBI announced no evidence of a criminal cause and withdrew from active involvement. The CIA animation presented in that context was a reconstruction of possible witness perceptions.
-  NTSB adopts probable cause: On 23 August 2000, the NTSB adopted its tank-explosion finding. It identified excessive voltage through fuel-quantity wiring as the most likely ignition route, while leaving the actual source uncertain.
-  Petition seeks reconsideration: On 19 June 2013, the TWA 800 Project submitted radar, witness and physical-evidence arguments challenging the NTSB findings. Some petitioners had participated in the original investigation; the submission was not an official Board dissent.
-  Petitioners present their case: On 10 January 2014, NTSB staff heard the petitioners' presentation, including an eyewitness. The meeting was a listening session for the petition, not adoption of a new cause.
-  NTSB denies the petition: On 2 July 2014, the NTSB denied reconsideration in full after reviewing the radar arguments and 20 additional interview summaries. It also answered physical-evidence and process allegations in an attachment.
-  Reconstruction retirement announced: On 22 February 2021, the NTSB announced retirement of its physical reconstruction from training use and plans for 3D documentation. It said the wreckage would be destroyed in keeping with its agreement with families.
-  Renewed records advocacy in 2026: On 18 July 2026, Judicial Watch announced a documentary and receipt of FBI records. The opened extract records early leads and faxes claiming responsibility; it does not verify an attack or replace the technical investigation.
-  New archive finding aid published: The Clinton Library finding aid updated on 28 August 2026 describes newly processed TWA 800 records. It is an archival development; no new NTSB probable-cause determination was found in the 2024–2026 search through 7 November 2026.

### Not known

- The exact electrical fault, point of entry into the CWT, ignition location and ignition energy were not established; fuel-quantity wiring is the NTSB's most-likely route, not certainty.
- No FDR/CVR data exist after power loss. Exact nose separation time, main-body pitch/roll, engine thrust, peak altitude and final water-impact time are not measured. Published post-cutoff model curves were read but no reliable numerical ordinate series was transcribed.
- The 150 track points are 78 radar position observations and 72 revised one-second FDR row samples. They are separate observations, not 150 complete aircraft states. The FDR had no positional parameters; no coordinates were invented for its samples and no interpolation was baked into this file.
- All 72 FDR rows use first listed parameter values within the second; the underlying system samples asynchronously at roughly 0.671875-second frame intervals. Exact subsecond time of each selected value is not recoverable from the labelled printed row alone.
- Some raw recorder values are anomalous. In particular IAS 455 kt at 00:30:21 UTC should not drive physical motion. No unsupported filter or corrected replacement number is supplied.
- The original NTSB X/Y-to-geographic projection and geographic datum were not fully specified in the radar table. The WGS84 local inverse used here is approximate. Radar coordinates rounded to 0.0001° do not imply that precision of measurement; cross-site offsets can be much larger.
- The published Islip altitude codes are retained in radar method strings, but their absolute MSL correction was not verified. Radar alt_ft is therefore null. FDR altitude is the NTSB's already-corrected MSL value; no second correction is applied.
- No exact individual final debris-impact positions, red/yellow/green polygons or defensible zone centroids were transcribed from the chart database. Their evidence-point coordinates are null. The three zones are areas; they must not be displayed as precise point impacts.
- Water depth at each debris zone and accident-site sea state/wave height were not established from the opened accident sources. NOAA buoy 44025 supplies actual regional wave observations, but its 1996 mooring position was not verified and its measurements cannot be assigned to the accident site.
- Cloud type and cloud tops for the surface station layers are unknown. GOES only constrains nearby top heights, not the same cloud parcels. Surface bases have been converted from AGL with current field elevations and must not be treated as precise 1996 aircraft-level clouds.
- No reliable local turbulence measurement was found. Lightning networks had incomplete offshore/all-flash coverage, so absence of detected local lightning is not coded as an absolute false.
- The full planned navigation waypoint list was not found in the opened final report and dispatch-release packet. Current airport reference coordinates are from a secondary dataset, not historical runway/gate coordinates.
- Accident-specific geometric height, numeric wing sweep and exact paint details on 17 July 1996 were not verified. Reference dimensions are used; wing_sweep_deg is null. The FAA-hosted N93119 photograph is undated.
- The precise source and deposition date of the three explosive residues remain unknown. A dog-training exercise and recovery handling are possibilities, not proved explanations for every detection.
- The anonymous 30-knot surface radar track was not positively identified. Lack of identity is not proof of a missile boat; the reviewed record contains no verified weapon launch or attacker.
- Individual witness observation coordinates and exact observation times are not transcribed here. Accounts are carefully marked as paraphrases; original FBI documents are often unreviewed interview summaries rather than verbatim witness transcripts.
- Most investigation/petition/recovery milestones have dates but no verified UTC clock time. Their t_utc is null and the date is stated in the detail rather than inventing midnight. Null-timed breakup events carry explicit estimated time ranges.
- The 2024–2026 search found renewed FOIA advocacy, a 2026 FBI extract and a newly processed Clinton Library collection, but no new official probable-cause determination. The full 415-page FOIA production and approximately 1,900-page archive collection were not read; their uninspected contents and any still-pending legal outcome remain unknown.

## EgyptAir 990, 31 October 1999

A Boeing 767 dived, briefly climbed, then struck the Atlantic, killing all 217 aboard. U.S. investigators blamed the relief first officer’s control movements; Egyptian investigators disputed that conclusion and sought further mechanical investigation.

Aboard: 217. Died: 217.

### Explanations

**NTSB control inputs** (official finding; US National Transportation Safety Board; Boeing’s technical submission also rejected a mechanical cause.). The NTSB found that the relief first officer’s flight-control inputs caused the departure from cruise and subsequent impact. It inferred opposing pilot inputs during the elevator split and attributed engine shutdown to the relief first officer. The reason for his actions was not determined; a specific suicide or revenge motive is not an established finding.

**ECAA elevator failure hypothesis** (official dissent; Egyptian Civil Aviation Authority investigation team, with EgyptAir technical submissions.). ECAA said the evidence could not establish one causal sequence and regarded an elevator-system defect as plausible and likely. Its main scenario involved a latent right-elevator actuator fault followed by another actuator jam, with pilot recovery efforts and possible later elevator loss. It called for further investigation rather than proving a particular failed component.

**Possible traffic avoidance** (minority; ECAA and EgyptAir as an unresolved alternative, not a demonstrated occurrence.). ECAA retained the possibility that the relief first officer manoeuvred to avoid a perceived collision or another emergency. It questioned unidentified radar returns and incomplete access to radar evaluation data. It did not establish an intruding aircraft, missile or collision.

## Air New Zealand 901, 28 November 1979

A sightseeing aircraft struck Mount Erebus after an unannounced route change and loss of terrain contrast. The collision sequence is established, but two official inquiries differed over the roles of crew decisions and airline failures.

Aboard: 257. Died: 257.

### Explanations

**Low-level continuation** (official finding; Chief Inspector of Air Accidents Ron Chippindale, Report 79-139 (1980)). The captain continued at low level towards poor surface and horizon definition without adequate certainty about position. Chippindale treated this decision and the inability to detect rising terrain as probable cause, while also documenting airline and regulatory failures.

**Airline systems and whiteout** (official finding; Justice Peter Mahon, Royal Commission (1981); causation accepted in the 2019 government apology). The airline changed the navigation track without telling the crew, who believed it led down McMurdo Sound. A clear-air whiteout illusion then concealed the rising slope ahead. Mahon held that defective airline administration was the dominant cause and that the flight crew made no contributory error.

**Organised post-accident deception** (rejected; Justice Mahon, especially Royal Commission paras. 45–54, 338–377). Mahon inferred a coordinated effort to conceal airline failures after the crash. The Privy Council rejected the supporting reasoning and fairness of that finding.

**Flying in cloud** (rejected; Early investigative speculation described by Mahon, paras. 24–28). One early explanation was that the aircraft had become enveloped in cloud at low altitude. The inquiries instead found evidence of clear air beneath the overcast; lack of visible terrain contrast did not require immersion in cloud.

## Air France 447, 1 June 2009

Unreliable speed readings preceded loss of control, a sustained loss of wing lift, and ocean impact. Recovered recorders establish the loss of control and stall sequence, while corporate legal responsibility remains contested.

Aboard: 228. Died: 228.

### Explanations

**Loss of control and stall** (official finding; BEA safety investigation). Temporary airspeed disagreement most likely followed ice-crystal obstruction of the Pitot probes. Control inputs caused a climb and exit from the flight envelope, followed by a stall that the crew did not diagnose or recover from; training, feedback and cockpit-interface weaknesses contributed.

**Airbus disputes criminal liability** (minority; Airbus, in its 21 May 2026 statement). Airbus contests the conviction and announced a cassation appeal. It cites the 2019 dismissal and 2023 acquittal as earlier decisions in its favour; this is a legal defence, not an alternative physical flight path.

**Early lightning explanation** (rejected; Air France’s communications director, as reported on 1 June 2009). Early public statements suggested a lightning strike could have caused the loss. This preceded recovery of the recorders and is not supported as the accident’s causal explanation by the completed investigation.

## Japan Air Lines 123, 12 August 1985

An incorrectly repaired pressure wall failed, damaging the tail and controls before a mountain crash. Wreckage and flight recordings support the repair failure explanation; questions remain about rescue decisions and why the repair went wrong.

Aboard: 524. Died: 520.

### Explanations

**Improper repair and fatigue** (official finding; AAIC; retained by JTSB and summarized by FAA). An improperly made 1978 bulkhead splice concentrated load in a single rivet row. Fatigue cracks grew until the bulkhead ruptured; escaping cabin air damaged the aft structure and vertical fin, and hydraulic losses removed primary flight control.

**Flutter before bulkhead failure** (rejected; Critics described and answered in JTSB's 2011 explanatory document). This view proposes that structural weakness or flutter first damaged the vertical fin, with the bulkhead damage occurring later. It questions a rapid-decompression interpretation of the cabin observations.

**External object or missile** (fringe; Writer Tohko Aoyama and other external-object proponents; described critically in s3 and discussed by Tomotsune). Proponents suggest collision with a military target aircraft or missile, sometimes linked to allegations of concealment. No verified foreign object or explosive evidence establishing that cause was found in the sources examined.

**Earlier rescue was possible** (credible alternative; Former USAF navigator Michael Antonucci; some survivors, families and later critics). Antonucci recalled that US helicopter personnel offered to descend nearby but were ordered away. This is a credible issue for rescue-history review; the number of additional people an earlier response could have saved is not established.

## Star Dust, 2 August 1947

Star Dust struck Tupungato in cloud; glacier ice concealed the wreckage for decades. Wreckage established a mountain collision after a navigation error; the precise descent and final radio word remain unexplained.

Aboard: 11. Died: 11.

### Explanations

**Early descent in cloud** (official finding; Argentine JIAAC, Disposición 43/00; investigation presented in BBC/NOVA). JIAAC concluded that the crew descended before reaching the intended position because they miscalculated groundspeed. It associated the error with mountain-wave and jet-stream headwinds, cloud and use of the central route in poor weather.

**Weather forced the descent** (minority; Some former BSAA pilots, as reported secondhand with a citation to Jay Rayner, Star Dust Falling, pp. 215–216). These pilots reportedly doubted that the captain would start descending without a positive indication that he had crossed the mountains. They suggested that strong winds might have brought the aircraft down by another mechanism; a specific validated alternative path was not located.

**Severe icing possible** (official finding; UK Ministry of Civil Aviation, Chief Inspector’s 1947 opinion). The first inquiry could not determine the cause while no wreckage had been found. It explicitly kept severe icing open, rather than finding icing proved.

**Airport call sign misread** (credible alternative; Martin Colwell, SAR Technology, and retired radio operator Lawrence Hooper). They propose that the word was actually SCTI followed by AR, with the Morse letter spacing misread. This is a published decoding hypothesis, not an accepted official translation.

**Other Morse spacing readings** (minority; Radio specialists and viewers whose proposals BBC Horizon reviewed). Proposals include a V attention signal, END and an AR sign-off, or a damaged ETA LATE message. BBC regarded Morse-based explanations as more plausible than invented acronyms, while leaving the word unresolved.

**Valparaíso call proposed** (minority; John L. Scherer, The North Texas Skeptic, December 2010). Scherer proposes that the crew meant VALP and were trying to contact or divert toward Valparaíso. His article calls this a solution, but no official acceptance or contemporary diversion record was found.

**DESCENT or invented acronym** (rejected; Viewers and correspondents discussed by BBC Horizon; not an official finding). STENDEC is an English-letter anagram of DESCENT, leading to suggestions of hypoxia or a scrambled descent message. Others invented phrases whose initials spell the word; BBC’s review found those readings implausible.

**Sabotage or diplomatic attack** (rejected; Rumours discussed in BBC/NOVA and contemporary coverage; no identified evidential proponent in the opened primary reports). Speculation included deliberate destruction connected with diplomatic correspondence or airline rivalry. These rumours grew while the aircraft was missing, but were not established by physical evidence.

**Alien abduction** (fringe; UFO speculation described and rejected by BBC Horizon/NOVA). Some accounts attached alien abduction to the disappearance and the unexplained word. The claim has no supporting accident evidence and is included only because it became part of the story’s public history.

### What happened

- 1947-08-02 13:00:00 Route forecast issued: The forecast described snowstorms, obscured passes and moderate to intense mountain turbulence. The crew had been briefed to avoid the central route in bad weather.
- 1947-08-02 13:46:00 Departure from Morón: Five crew and six passengers left Presidente Rivadavia for Los Cerrillos on flight CS59. The flight plan went via Mendoza.
- 1947-08-02 15:07:00 First preserved position report: The crew reported 33°55′S, 62°33′W at 10,000 ft, course 286°, and estimated Santiago at 17:30 UTC. The printed speed has a later unit dispute.
- 1947-08-02 16:00:00 Second preserved position report: The reported position was 33°25′S, 65°30′W at 10,000 ft, course 282°. The ETA remained 17:30 UTC.
- 1947-08-02 16:45:00 Destination weather transmitted: JIAAC says the Los Cerrillos weather summary was transmitted to the aircraft. It described altocumulus and improving visibility of 5–8 nautical miles.
- 1947-08-02 17:00:00 Climb reported near Mendoza: The crew reported 20,000 ft and a climb toward 24,000 ft at 32°50′S, 68°30′W. They revised their Santiago ETA to 17:43 UTC.
- 1947-08-02 17:33:00 Arrival estimate revised: The aircraft estimated Santiago at 17:45 UTC. Its actual position and altitude are absent from the preserved entry.
- 1947-08-02 17:41:00 Final message received: The aircraft confirmed an ETA of 17:45 UTC and ended with the sequence shown below. This is an official report transcription of a radio word, not cockpit audio.
- 1947-08-02 17:41:00 Operator requests clarification: The Santiago operator said reception was strong and clear but fast. The aircraft repeated the same word twice; the report supplies no separate timestamps for these exchanges.

-  Chilean military aircraft join search: On 5 August 1947, the historical account reproduced by JIAAC says three Chilean A-24 aircraft joined the search. No time of day is supplied.
-  Tupungato searched without success: On 7 August 1947, a US mission DC-4 searched the Cordón del Plata and Tupungato according to the account reproduced in the Argentine inquiry. No confirmed wreckage was found.
-  British search mission sent: The retrospective account dates a British official mission to 18 August 1947. It describes searches in San Juan and Mendoza.
-  British search suspended: The same account dates suspension of the British mission search to 21 August 1947. The aircraft remained missing.
-  Investigator visits the region: The UK report says the vicinity was visited on 31 August 1947. The wreckage had not been located.
-  First inquiry leaves cause open: The UK report was signed on 22 December 1947 and published in 1948. It could not establish a cause without wreckage and retained severe icing as a possibility.
-  Climbers find engine remains: In 1998, two mountaineers found aircraft remains on Tupungato. Los Andes later dates this to 26 August 1998; no original discovery log or exact first-find coordinates were obtained.
-  Wreckage identified on return expedition: On 19 January 2000, military and civilian searchers found wreckage and identification material. The Argentine inquiry also contains conflicting January/February dates for later visits; these are retained in conflicts.

-  Propeller and wheels examined: During the 2000 expedition, documentary investigators interpreted propeller damage as evidence that the recovered engine was rotating at impact. They interpreted the main-wheel condition as consistent with retracted gear; neither finding measures the state of all four engines.
-  Glacier movement explains concealment: In the 2000 investigation filmed by BBC/NOVA, glaciologist Juan Carlos Leiva explained transport within the ice and emergence in the lower melting zone. Investigator Carlos Bauza proposed that an impact-triggered avalanche first buried the aircraft.
-  Argentine findings publicly announced: La Nación on 8 July 2000 reports that the findings were announced the previous day. JIAAC attributed the collision to descent before the intended position, with erroneous groundspeed calculation and adverse mountain weather.
-  DNA identification reported: Los Andes later dates the forensic comparison announcement to 5 September 2002 and reports eight identifications or identifying approximations. The original forensic report was not obtained, so a final independently verified identification count remains unknown.
-  Later message claims remain unconfirmed: Searches covering 2024–7 November 2026 located renewed coverage, including a June 2026 Popular Mechanics article. No new official reconstruction or accepted STENDEC solution was located; current retellings are not new flight evidence.

## Lady Be Good, 4 April 1943

After overshooting its Libyan base, the crew parachuted into the desert; all nine died. The wreck, remains, and diaries explain the loss; precise navigation failures and one crew member's whereabouts remain unresolved.

Aboard: 9. Died: 9.

### Explanations

**Reciprocal bearing and overflight** (official finding; Graves Registration reconstruction as quoted by Quartermaster history; Joseph Portney; later institutional histories). The bomber passed its base and continued inland while its crew believed it was still returning over the Mediterranean. The quoted official reconstruction proposes a reciprocal-bearing error, followed by fuel depletion and bailout; it does not supply a continuous measured route.

**Unanswered Benghazi radio call** (credible alternative; Mario Martinez, drawing on retrospective interviews and letters). Martinez gives greater causal weight to an unanswered request at Benghazi radio headquarters, distinct from Benina and Soluch. He argues that suspicion of a German aircraft prevented useful help from reaching the crew.

**Simulated final right turn** (minority; Ed Truthan, LadyBeGood.net flight-simulation study). A later simulator explores a trimmed, one-engine descent with a right turn. Its numerical output is a visualization hypothesis, not historical telemetry.

**Possible enemy damage** (minority; William G. Holder; discussed cautiously by Mario Martinez). A recovered engine reportedly contained damage or a fragment associated with a 20-mm projectile. Holder suggested possible enemy fire near the target; this does not establish when the damage occurred or that it caused the loss.

**Unidentified burial may be Moore** (minority; Mario Martinez and the former British convoy participant who contacted him). Unidentified remains photographed and buried in 1953 might have been the still-missing gunner. The proposed location is an approximate regional inference, not a recovered grave or accepted identification.

**Salvaged parts carried a curse** (fringe; Later folklore repeated by Holder and documentaries). Stories linked subsequent aircraft troubles to reused parts from the bomber. They are folklore, not a supported cause of this accident or of the later accidents.

### What happened

- 1943-04-04 11:50:00 Group departs Soluch: 4 April 1943: the group summary gives 1150 GMT as departure time. This is a group reference, not a verified individual takeoff time for Lady Be Good.
-  Lady Be Good takes off: 4 April: the engineer’s diary transcription gives takeoff as 15:10. Another military summary gives 14:50 and later accounts differ; the individual UTC time is unresolved.
-  Formation separates: 4 April: sand and poor visibility disrupted the formation. Lady Be Good returned separately from the other bombers.
- 1943-04-04 17:35:00 Group attacks Naples harbor: 4 April: the group report records bombing between 1735 and 1745 GMT at 23,500–25,000 feet. These values describe the attacking group and do not establish Lady Be Good’s position or altitude.
-  Return turn is logged: 4 April, 20:52 on an unspecified clock: Martinez reports a 140-degree departure-course entry in the navigator’s log. This is a later transcription; the original sheet was not obtained.
-  Engineer records bomb release: 4 April, 22:00 on an unspecified clock: the engineer records releasing bombs. The entry alone does not establish bombing Naples rather than jettisoning over water.
-  Crew requests radio help: Late 4 April or early 5 April: accounts describe a request for a bearing and a malfunctioning direction finder. The quoted Army reconstruction gives a 330-degree bearing from Benina; the original radio log was not recovered for this research.
-  Aircraft is heard near base: Around midnight on the witnesses’ clocks, personnel heard a bomber near Soluch and fired flares. Retrospective testimony identifies it as probably Lady Be Good; no exact overhead fix follows from the sound.
-  Benghazi call may go unanswered: Around midnight: Martinez says a separate Benghazi radio station failed to answer the pilot because an enemy aircraft was suspected. This contested account rests on later interviews, not a cockpit recording.
-  Fuel becomes depleted: Early 5 April by crew dating: the copilot records running out of fuel. Later examination found three propellers feathered and the fourth engine configured to operate.
-  Crew bails out: Early 5 April: the copilot records landing in the desert at 02:00; the engineer gives bailout at 02:10. These are unconverted crew clock readings, not verified UTC times.
-  Uncrewed bomber strikes desert: After abandonment, the bomber struck a gravel plain and skidded roughly 700 yards. Its fourth engine detached and its fuselage broke behind the wings; the exact impact time, speed and attitude are unknown.
- 1943-04-05 02:00:00 Group reports missing aircraft: The 5 April confirmation says its telegraphic summary was filed at 0200 GMT. Aircraft 64 with its pilot was unaccounted for; a second bomber was also still unaccounted for in this initial report.
-  Eight men begin walking: 5 April: eight men assembled without the bombardier and walked northwest. The copilot recorded a few rations and half a canteen of water, rationed by capful.
-  Walking and resting alternate: 6 April: the diary records resting at 11:30 until 17:00, then walking and resting through the night in a 15-minute walking, 5-minute resting pattern. The clock zone is unknown.
-  Crew reaches sand dunes: 8 April: the copilot records reaching dunes amid blowing sand. The men were weakening and their eyes were affected.
-  Three continue for help: The copilot places the separation on 9 April; the engineer mentions it under Saturday 10 April. The engineer and two gunners continued while five men remained behind.
-  Engineer makes final entry: 11 April: the engineer’s final entry says they were still struggling through dunes to find water. It does not give a location or establish when he died.
-  Copilot makes final entry: 12 April: the copilot’s last written entry reports no help and another cold night. Blank later diary spaces do not establish anyone’s exact time of death.
-  British patrol buries unidentified remains: February 1953: a later account says a British convoy photographed and buried unidentified remains while crossing the sand sea. Their proposed connection with the missing gunner was raised decades later and remains unproved.
-  Early aerial sighting is reported: 16 May 1958: Martinez reports a sighting by oil-survey personnel, followed by another on 15 June. Other accounts instead emphasize November 1958; discovery chronology remains conflicted.
-  November sighting enters later accounts: 9 November 1958 is the aerial-discovery date repeated in the 2021 physics article. This is retained alongside the May and June accounts rather than called the unique first sighting.
-  Oil surveyors reach the wreck: 27–28 February 1959: Bowerman, Sheridan and Martin reached and examined the bomber in the detailed later accounts. Institutional summaries often place the ground visit in March.
-  Military search begins: May 1959: Army mortuary and Air Force personnel investigated the empty wreck and began searching for the crew. Fuller and Neep were key Army investigators.
-  Boots reveal the northward trail: 16 June 1959 according to Lowery: searchers found flight boots about 19 miles north of the wreck. Further searches followed old vehicle tracks and found discarded equipment and parachute arrows.
-  Another arrow marker is found: 20 July 1959: an additional parachute-and-stone arrow was found during a return trip toward the wreck. Its mapped position is a later approximation.
-  First search closes without remains: 2 September 1959 in Lowery’s account: the search team was airlifted out after extensive effort. The working explanation was that the men’s remains had been covered by sand.
-  First investigation report is dated: 17 November 1959: the Quartermaster bibliography identifies a report by Fuller and Neep on the lost bomber and the 1959 search. The full report was not obtained.
-  Five crew members are found: 11 February 1960: oil workers found five crew members together, with equipment and the copilot’s diary. The 2024 documentary describes the discovery message arriving on 12 February.
-  Operation Climax expands the search: Spring 1960: a joint Army–Air Force operation used ground parties, aircraft and helicopters to seek the four still missing. It followed the February recovery.
-  A gunner is found farther north: 12 May 1960: oil workers found Shelley. Relative-distance accounts conflict, so his published recovery coordinate is kept separately from claims about miles walked.
-  Flight engineer is found: 17 May 1960: a helicopter search found Ripslinger. His diary was recovered; the published coordinate lies south of Shelley’s, contrary to some institutional retellings.
-  Final organized search ends: Late May 1960: Operation Climax ended with two men still missing. The later bombardier discovery was separate.
-  Second investigation report is dated: 20 June 1960: the Quartermaster bibliography identifies the final-search report by Fuller and Neep. Its text and appendices were not located in an accessible digital copy.
-  Bombardier is found near bailout area: 11 August 1960 in the detailed later accounts: the bombardier was found near the original descent area with a partially deployed parachute. Nearby abandoned flying equipment and spent flares identified the survivors’ assembly area.
-  Crew memorial window is dedicated: January 1961: a memorial stained-glass window was unveiled at Wheelus. It was later moved to the U.S. Air Force museum.
-  Components support materials research: April 1968: an RAF desert team recovered components for materials study. The wreck’s long exposure provided evidence about deterioration during storage.
-  Wreckage is moved for safekeeping: August 1994: Libyan authorities removed the remaining wreckage to Tobruk. The original crash-site coordinates therefore do not locate the surviving airframe today.
-  Moore photograph is assessed: 2001–2002: Martinez investigated a photograph from the 1953 convoy and sought an osteologist’s opinion. The photograph could not establish identity and did not close the missing gunner’s case.

### Not known

- Exact UTC accident date/time. Crew dating places bailout in the early hours of 5 April, but their clock zone was not established. Depending on that zone the UTC date could be 4 or 5 April. date_utc is therefore null; the mission began on 4 April UTC.
- Time convention: only explicitly labelled GMT in the primary group report is normalized to UTC (same historical civil time reference for this use). Unlabelled local clocks, diary times and day-only discoveries are not converted or padded with midnight timestamps. Null event times retain the date in detail and narrative order.
- No flight recorder, cockpit voice recorder, radar, ADS-B, ACARS or satellite-log time series was found or expected for this 1943 bomber. The nine main track stages are not a sampled trajectory and must not be interpolated as measured flight.
- Full 17 November 1959 and 20 June 1960 Quartermaster reports, appendices, original field notebooks and survey maps were not retrieved. Their existence, dates and authors were checked in the opened bibliography; conclusions used here are explicitly mediated through later accounts.
- The brief attribution to Graham Barlow could not be substantiated. The opened Quartermaster bibliography names Myron C. Fuller Jr. and Wesley A. Neep for both reports.
- No Hatton survival diary was located. The journals found and checked belong to copilot Toner and engineer Ripslinger. Woravka’s reported notebook is a separate item; no complete primary scan was obtained.
- Original navigator W6 sheet, all outbound positions and original ground radio logs were not obtained. The 20:52/140-degree entry is Martinez’s report of the sheet, not a newly read instrument record.
- Exact Italian turning point, bomb-release location, approach to the Libyan coast and inland flight course. A city or airfield center must not be used as proof the aircraft crossed that exact coordinate.
- Exact number, wording, order, timestamps, frequencies and receipt of Benina, Soluch and Benghazi radio calls. No verbatim radio quotation is supplied from retrospective recollection.
- Whether direction-finder malfunction, a reciprocal interpretation, ground non-response or several failures best explain continuing inland. A 330-degree bearing is not itself an aircraft heading or a two-dimensional fix.
- True versus magnetic reference for reported 140/150/330-degree values. No numerical declination correction has been applied.
- Individual aircraft altitude, airspeed, groundspeed, attitude, vertical speed, angle of attack and fuel quantities throughout the mission and descent. Group 23,500–25,000-foot bombing altitudes are not assigned to this aircraft.
- Actual trim, engine shutdown times, autopilot state, bailout sequence and instantaneous aircraft position for each jump. Final engine-control settings cannot establish when an engine stopped.
- Exact impact point versus final resting point, crash elevation and vertical terrain profile. The approximately 700-yard skid is reported, not a mapped polyline with endpoints.
- A modern surveyed wreck coordinate. The old astro-fix and later satellite-footprint interpretation differ. Wreckage was removed in 1994; its exact storage position and condition in November 2026 were not verified.
- Exact Soluch takeoff-strip center, threshold and elevation. The route uses a historical landing-ground vicinity from a secondary airfield study, not modern Benina coordinates or its airport codes.
- Precise Naples aimpoint, full authorized flight plan, turnpoints and diversion instructions; no geographic waypoint positions were invented.
- Measured cloud base/top/coverage, winds aloft, pressure, visibility, precipitation, turbulence, lightning and temperature along the flight. Absence of a report is not zero wind, clear sky or no precipitation.
- Lunar phase, elevation and illumination at bailout were not verified; no full-moon or moonless-night claim is made. Later simulator wind inputs and climatology are not observations for 5 April 1943.
- Exact walking line, daily positions and construction times for trail arrows. All seven plotted arrow positions come from Truthan’s model, not surveyed military fixes. Do not attach aircraft altitude or speed to this ground trail.
- Exact total walking distances, local dune detours, whether later recoveries shifted from the death positions, and precise times when individuals died. Diaries establish minimum survival, not a common death time.
- Moore’s remains and grave location. The approximate 28 N, 23 E proposal is not a confirmed discovery or identification; no accepted recovery was found in the 2024–2026 search.
- Cause, date and operational importance of the reported projectile fragment. Full laboratory documentation and a matching combat encounter were not located.
- Exact quantity of drinkable water and usable supplies left aboard at abandonment, and whether the crew could have found the wreck, used its radio or reached an oasis. Such survival outcomes are counterfactual, not established findings.
- Airframe fuselage diameter, numerical wing sweep definition, exact April 1943 underside color and paint standard were not verified. They remain null or explicitly qualified rather than estimated from generic model dimensions.
- No specific post-accident safety regulation demonstrably caused by this loss was found. Materials research and its use as a navigation/survival teaching case are documented; claimed rule changes without a primary record are omitted.
- 2024–2026 update search: the opened December 2024 PBS documentary and July 2026 retrospective supplied no verified new recovery, DNA identification or official finding. This is a bounded search result, not a guarantee that no unpublished development exists.
- Access limits: current USAF Museum pages and the Air University journal PDF returned 403; the Army museum host failed to resolve. Relevant archival images, AFHRA scans, a complete archived Air University article and archived Quartermaster pages were opened instead. Failed URLs are not counted as opened sources.
