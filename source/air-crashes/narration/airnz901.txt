Auckland, the morning of November twenty-eighth, 1979. 257 people board a sightseeing flight to Antarctica and back. Before it leaves, the last waypoint in its route is moved east. The crew are not told.

Five hours later, near McMurdo, a forecaster reports low cloud around 2,000 feet. The captain plans to go lower, to show the passengers the ice.

The captain sees a break in the cloud, and circles down through it, twice.

The autopilot picks up the programmed route again. Because of the changed waypoint, that line now leads to the northern slopes of Mount Erebus.

They settle at 1,500 feet. White ground, white cloud. In this light, a rising snow slope and a far horizon can look the same.

The captain decides to leave. "We're twenty six miles north. We'll have to climb out of this."

They cross the coast. The ground starts to rise.

Six and a half seconds before impact, the ground warning sounds.

All 257 aboard died. The first investigation mainly blamed the decision to fly low. A Royal Commission then blamed the route change and the airline's procedures, and cleared the crew. The airline apologized for its treatment of families in 2009. In 2019, the government and the airline apologized again.
