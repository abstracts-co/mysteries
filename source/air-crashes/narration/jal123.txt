Tokyo, August twelfth, 1985. Japan Air Lines 123 takes off for Osaka with 524 people. Seven years earlier, this aircraft struck its tail on landing. The repair to its rear pressure wall was done incorrectly, and inspectors missed it.

Twelve minutes in, at 24,000 feet, a loud sound. The pressure wall has failed. Air bursts into the tail, and tears away much of the fin and the hydraulic power that moves the controls.

The captain asks to return to Tokyo.

The aircraft begins to climb and fall, and to sway from side to side. The controls cannot stop it. In the cabin, the oxygen masks are down.

The captain tells controllers the aircraft cannot be controlled.

The crew has little left to work with but engine power. The landing gear comes down.

The sun has set. Power comes back, and the aircraft descends faster, over the mountains.

Speed falls as low as 108 knots.

The crew asks where they are. Controllers tell them Tokyo and the American base at Yokota are both ready.

The nose drops into a steep descent.

A ground warning. Engine power comes up, and the descent briefly stops. Then the right wing strikes trees on a ridge.

It stayed in the air for 32 minutes after the failure. 520 people died, the most ever in a single aircraft accident. Rescuers did not reach the site until the next morning, and found four survivors, all seated in the rear of the cabin. Afterward, the 747 tail and hydraulic systems were changed to protect against a failure like this.
