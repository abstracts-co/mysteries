New York, 1:20 in the morning, October thirty-first, 1999. EgyptAir 990 leaves for Cairo with 217 people aboard. Its crew includes a relief first officer, for the long crossing.

It levels off at cruising height, over the dark Atlantic. The relief first officer has taken the right seat.

The captain says he will be away for a moment, and leaves the cockpit.

The autopilot disengages. No warning sounds. The relief first officer repeats a quiet phrase. "I rely on God."

The engine power levers move to idle. Both tail controls move down, and the nose starts to drop.

The captain's voice is recorded as he comes back. "What's happening? What's happening?"

The nose is about 40 degrees below level. The aircraft is falling at an estimated 39,000 feet a minute, and nearing the speed of sound.

Now the two tail controls split. The left moves to raise the nose, the right to push it down. Fuel to both engines is cut off. Who moved those controls is not recorded.

The captain calls out. "Pull with me."

With the engines stopped, the recorders lose power. From here, only radar remains.

Radar suggests the aircraft climbed back to about 25,000 feet and turned. Then it descended again.

It struck the ocean at about this time. All 217 people aboard died.

The two countries read the same recorders and reached different conclusions. The United States blamed the relief first officer's control movements, and did not determine why he made them. Egypt rejected a deliberate act, left the cause unresolved, and considered an elevator defect likely. The words on the recording do not settle it.
