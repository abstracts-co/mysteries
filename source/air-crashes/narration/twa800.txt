New York, a summer evening, July seventeenth, 1996. After a delay at the gate, TWA 800 lifts off for Paris with 230 people aboard.

The sun has just set. Climbing along the coast of Long Island, the crew is told to hold at 13,000 feet.

The captain notices something on the panel. "Look at that crazy fuel flow indicator there on number four." No link to what follows was ever established.

Controllers clear a climb to 15,000 feet. Power comes up. It is the last radio exchange.

Twelve seconds past 8:31. A brief, loud sound, and the recorders stop.

The explosion began in the fuel tank between the wings. Within about five seconds, the nose of the aircraft separated. Everything you see from here is from models, because no recording exists.

Without its nose, the rest of the aircraft kept flying. Models show it climbing for a short time, to perhaps 15,000 or 16,000 feet.

About half a minute after the explosion, the outer wings failed. Burning fuel lit the sky above the water.

Hundreds of people saw it from the shore, from boats and from the air. A nearby airline captain radioed that he had seen an explosion.

The main wreckage hit the ocean less than a minute after the explosion. All 230 people aboard died.

Some witnesses described a streak of light rising, and many people came to believe a missile had been fired. The FBI found no evidence of a crime. The NTSB, working from wreckage rebuilt in a hangar, concluded the tank exploded, probably from excess voltage in fuel gauge wiring, though the source was never found. Petitioners challenged that in 2013. The Board denied the petition. New rules now reduce flammable vapor in fuel tanks.
