Lae, New Guinea, July second, 1937. Amelia Earhart and her navigator, Fred Noonan, take off for Howland Island, a strip of land about two kilometers long, thousands of kilometers away. Only a few legs of a flight around the world remain.

Seven hours out, Lae hears its last report. They are above the clouds.

Through the night, the Coast Guard ship Itasca waits at Howland. Its radio picks up a voice through static, too faint to use.

Morning. Earhart asks Itasca for a bearing, and estimates she is 200 miles out. About half an hour later, she says 100 miles. Itasca cannot get a direction on that frequency.

She reports flying at 1,000 feet, and low on fuel. She cannot see the ship.

She hears the ship's signals now, but cannot use them to find its direction. The ship is sending smoke, and calling. Neither can find the other.

Her last clear message is entered in the log. "We are on the line 157 337." She gives no point of reference. Then nothing that can be understood.

The Navy search found no trace and concluded they were lost at sea. Others believe that line led them to Nikumaroro, 650 kilometers to the southeast, where bones were found in 1940, then lost. Neither idea has been proven. A sonar image in 2024 proved to be rock. A lagoon target in 2026 was judged likely to be tree debris. The aircraft has never been found.
