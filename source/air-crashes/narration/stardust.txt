Buenos Aires, August second, 1947. The airliner Star Dust leaves for Santiago, with six passengers and five crew. Forecasts warn of snowstorms and cloud over the Andes.

The crew reports its position by Morse code, flying at 10,000 feet. There is no radar, and no recorder. These reports are nearly all that survive.

Near Mendoza, they report climbing toward 24,000 feet to cross the mountains.

They revise their arrival to 17:45. Investigators later inferred they were in cloud. No position was reported.

Four minutes before they expect to land, the Santiago operator receives the last message. It ends with one word. S, T, E, N, D, E, C.

Reception is strong and clear, but fast. The operator asks them to repeat it. The aircraft sends the same word twice more. No one has ever established what it means.

Star Dust was still in cloud when it struck Mount Tupungato. All eleven aboard died. They were not where they thought they were.

Searches found nothing, and the aircraft became a legend. In 1998, climbers found an engine on the glacier. The ice had carried the wreckage downhill, for more than fifty years. Argentine investigators concluded an incorrect speed calculation and bad weather led the crew to descend too early. The final word is still unexplained.
