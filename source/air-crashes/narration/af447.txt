Rio de Janeiro. The evening of May thirty-first, 2009. Air France 447 leaves for Paris with 228 people aboard.

Three hours out over the Atlantic, the crew reports its position. Soon after, the aircraft passes the edge of radar coverage.

Ahead is the band of storms that circles the equator. The captain goes to rest. Two copilots now have the controls.

The flying copilot warns the cabin crew. More turbulence is coming.

They turn left, probably to avoid weather on the radar.

Ten minutes past two. The autopilot disconnects. Ice crystals have most likely blocked the speed sensors, and the speed readings no longer agree.

The flight-control mode changes, and the automatic protection against a stall is gone. The flying copilot pulls the nose up.

The aircraft climbs at nearly 7,000 feet a minute.

Within half a minute, the left speed display is reliable again. Nobody calls for the procedure written for lost speed readings.

The stall warning sounds continuously now. The nose stays up.

At about 38,000 feet the climb ends. The wings can no longer provide enough lift, and the aircraft begins to descend with its nose still raised.

The captain returns to the cockpit. The crew does not agree on what is happening.

Then the stall warning goes silent, because the system rejects its readings when measured speed falls too low. The silence does not mean recovery.

Ten thousand feet. Both copilots are now moving their controls at once, one mostly nose down, the other mostly alternating.

About 2,000 feet above the sea, the ground warnings begin.

The recording ends. The aircraft struck the ocean in one piece, nose up, falling at nearly 11,000 feet a minute.

It took about two years to find the wreckage, 3,900 meters down. The recorders helped investigators trace confusion, an unrecognized stall, and the likely icing of speed sensors. Speed probes were replaced, and pilot training changed. In 2026, a Paris appeal court convicted Airbus and Air France of involuntary homicide. Airbus has said it will seek a further review.
